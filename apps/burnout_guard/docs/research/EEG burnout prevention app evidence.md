# Measure fatigue, anchor burnout in questionnaires

The evidence supports a narrower app than "EEG burnout prevention." Four dry temporal/ear electrodes can reasonably track **acute, within-day mental fatigue and drowsiness**: the theta and alpha rises are meta-analytically large. They cannot detect or predict **chronic burnout**, which has no validated EEG marker, and the "stress" signal at temporal sites is mostly jaw-muscle noise. The parts of the product with solid evidence are mostly not EEG. Micro-breaks reduce fatigue by about a third of a standard deviation. Slow breathing has small but reliable stress effects. Free, validated questionnaires (the Burnout Assessment Tool, the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, Need for Recovery, WHO-5) have published cut-offs and can anchor a multi-week risk view. The legal picture points the same way. EEG-derived data is treated as high-risk everywhere. The EU AI Act bans emotion inference from biometric data in the workplace but explicitly exempts fatigue. Claims about "burnout" or "stress" carry medical-device and AI Act risk. The defensible product is therefore a local-only, employee-only tool with four parts: EEG measures fatigue and alertness, a hybrid scheduler prompts breaks, short questionnaires carry the burnout signal, and the employer cannot technically see the data. Several pieces still lack validation: test-retest reliability of the headphones, EEG-confirmed recovery after a short break, and any individual burnout-prediction algorithm. The app should state these gaps openly rather than paper over them.

## Theta tracks fatigue robustly, while temporal "stress" mostly measures the jaw

The strongest EEG finding is a fatigue finding. A meta-analysis of 21 studies found that mental fatigue produced a **large theta increase (g = 1.03) and a large but more variable alpha increase (g = 0.85)**, with only small changes in delta and beta. The authors called theta a "robust biomarker" and alpha a second-line marker for individual variability ([Tran et al. 2020](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/the-influence-of-mental-fatigue-on-brain-activity-evidence-from-a/)). This matters for the scoring engine, which currently computes fatigue as (theta+alpha)/beta. Beta barely moves with fatigue, so the denominator adds little real signal. At temporal sites it adds noise instead: clenching the jaw lowers the "fatigue" score and relaxing raises it.

Two complications prevent naive use. First, normalisation choices can flip the result. In a 54-minute driving study, total broadband power rose with time on task, so absolute theta trended up while **relative frontal theta fell (η² ≈ 0.32)** ([Wascher et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00205/full)). Second, alpha drifts on its own. Within a single session, **alpha peak frequency slows and alpha power rises with time on task**, which challenges the assumption that spectra stay stable over an hour ([Benwell et al. 2019](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/frequency-and-power-of-human-alpha-oscillations-drift-systematica/)). IAF slowing is a plausible secondary fatigue marker, but it is not specific to fatigue, and an IAF calibrated at the start of the session goes stale. Theta/alpha "workload" indices are weaker still. On a 48-subject consumer dataset, binary workload classification reached only **about 60% from single bands and about 70% from ratios**, and the authors note that "minimal research exists to validate" these indices ([Raufi & Longo 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12937)). Theta also appears in both "load" and "fatigue," so presenting them as independent dimensions misleads users.

The "stress" index (20–30 Hz over alpha) has the weakest footing. Studies of pharmacologically paralysed subjects show that scalp EEG diverges from EMG-free EEG at **about 20 Hz**, that contamination worsens above 25 Hz and away from the vertex, and that no cleaning method fully removed EMG from beta or gamma without dense arrays ([PMC9480942](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9480942)). T3/T4 sit directly over the temporalis muscle. A high-beta index there mainly responds to chewing, talking and clenching. Frontal alpha asymmetry, the other popular stress and affect marker, needs F3/F4 electrodes this device lacks. A multiverse analysis of five studies also found **no relationship between frontal alpha asymmetry and depression** ([Kołodziej et al., eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/60595)).

Hardware validation narrows things further. Against a research amplifier, PSBD Headphones (electrodes at C3, C4, TP9, TP10, a layout matching this device's variant, though identity with Neiry hardware is unconfirmed) correlated **r = 0.72 for temporal alpha but only 0.33 for theta and 0.47 for high beta**. The headphones also showed spurious spectral spikes near 16 and 34 Hz, inside the beta bands the app uses ([Sensors 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/24/8108)). A second study found alpha log-PSD correlated **r = 0.87** with gel EEG but flagged low-frequency artefacts ([Frontiers Neurosci 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2024.1326139)). Neither paper measured day-to-day test-retest reliability. That gap is the main reason absolute values should not be compared across days. Ear-EEG works best for drowsiness: in-ear EEG separated wake from light sleep at **80% versus 86.8% for scalp** ([Nakamura et al. 2018](https://www.commsp.ee.ic.ac.uk/~mandic/research/EEG_BCI/TN_DPM_et_all_Ear-EEG_Drowsiness_IJCNN_2018.pdf)). In a monotonous one-hour drive, ear-EEG showed a fatigue-related theta rise (d = 1.3) and **70% classification accuracy versus 74% for scalp**. Beta was excluded because of EMG, and T7/T8 channels were discarded because of artefacts ([Yarici et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06406)). These accuracies are likely optimistic, because both studies used validation schemes that leak information between training and test data.

Chronic burnout is a different problem. The largest recent study compared 49 workers with burnout and 49 matched controls using 256-channel EEG. It found only **reduced 11–13 Hz coherence with eyes open**, and no power differences in any band. The authors call this a possible neurobiological basis, not a biomarker ([Frontiers Hum Neurosci 2025](https://frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2025.1481760/full)). Older small studies report lower alpha peak frequency and reduced beta at rest ([van Luijtelaar et al. 2010](https://www.doi.org/10.1176/JNP.2010.22.2.208)) or reduced resting alpha power ([Golonka et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6701350/)). These point the *opposite* way from the acute-fatigue pattern, where alpha rises. Summing daily fatigue scores therefore does not produce a burnout tracker.

| Evidence tier | Signal on this hardware |
|---|---|
| Strong | Theta rise with mental fatigue; alpha rise (more variable); EMG dominance above ~20–25 Hz at lateral sites; alpha measurable on dry headphones |
| Moderate | IAF slowing and alpha drift over a session; ear-EEG drowsiness detection (~80%) |
| Weak / unvalidated | Theta/alpha workload; beta/alpha "attention"; high-beta/alpha "stress"; frontal asymmetry; any chronic-burnout EEG marker; day-to-day reliability of the headphones |

## Micro-breaks lift energy, not output, and earlier breaks recover more

The best break evidence is a meta-analysis of 22 samples (N = 2,335). Micro-breaks of 10 minutes or less raised **vigor (d = 0.36)** and reduced **fatigue (d = 0.35)**, but did not significantly improve performance overall (d = 0.16). For cognitively demanding tasks the performance effect was effectively zero (d = −0.09). Break length moderated performance only: longer breaks gave bigger performance gains, but well-being effects held regardless of length ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)). Two implications follow. Short 1–5 minute breaks are justified as a fatigue intervention. The app should not market breaks as a productivity boost for knowledge work.

Named schedules have thin support. Only Pomodoro-style fixed intervals have a controlled test. In a one-day study of 87 students, fixed 24/6 or 12/3 schedules produced **less fatigue and better concentration and motivation than self-regulated breaks, with the same task completion** ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)). The famous 52/17 rule comes from a 2014 analysis by a time-tracking vendor that was never peer-reviewed. The vendor's own 2021 rerun found **112/26** instead ([Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-desktime-52-17-work-break-productivity-observational-data/)). The 90-minute ultradian rule circulates in business media without controlled workplace trials ([Inc.](https://www.inc.com/wanda-thibodeaux/why-working-in-90-minute-intervals-is-powerful-for-your-body-and-job-according-t.html)). The most concrete regulatory guidance is the UK HSE's: **"5 to 10 minutes every hour is better than 20 minutes every 2 hours,"** and users should ideally choose when to break ([HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/Msd/dse/work-routine.htm)).

Timing and autonomy matter more than the exact schedule. A diary study of 959 breaks found that **mid-morning breaks restored far more energy, concentration and motivation than late-afternoon ones**, and that preferred activities recovered best ([Hunter & Wu via APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835)). In a lunch-break study, freely chosen relaxation produced the lowest end-of-day fatigue. Working or socialising through lunch raised fatigue *unless the person chose it* ([Trougakos et al. via EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/768564)). Workers who spent lunch on their phones reported similar detachment to walkers but **more afternoon emotional exhaustion**, although the data were correlational ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/put-it-away-spending-work-breaks-your-smartphone-not-rejuvenating)). These findings support three practices: prompt the first break proactively instead of waiting for strong EEG fatigue, let the user choose the activity, and discourage phone breaks.

Field data set realistic expectations. In a three-week study of 25 information workers receiving 1,599 break prompts, **40% were accepted, 34% declined and 26% ignored**. Declines were mostly "busy working" (40.6%), "in a meeting" (18.9%) or "just had a break" (15.2%). Actual breaks ran longer than intended, averaging 5.9 versus 4.4 minutes ([Luo et al. CHI 2018](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)). Interruptions at low-cost task boundaries resumed in about **464 ms versus 1,702 ms** at high-cost boundaries ([Iqbal & Bailey 2006](https://interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI06-p741-iqbal.pdf)). Timing prompts with a receptivity model raised just-in-time response by **38%**, while the control arm's responsiveness decayed over time ([Künzler et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)). Together these point to a just-in-time adaptive intervention (JITAI) design. Such a design defines decision points (when the app may prompt), tailoring variables (EEG fatigue and receptivity) and decision rules, and treats "do nothing" as a valid option. For eye strain, 20-20-20 reminders reduced symptoms in 29 computer users, but **benefits vanished a week after reminders stopped** ([Talens-Estarelles et al.](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effects-of-breaks-on-digital-eye-strain-dry-eye-and-binocular/)). The hardest ceiling comes from WHO/ILO: working **55 or more hours a week carries a 35% higher stroke risk and a 17% higher ischaemic heart disease mortality risk** than 35–40 hours ([WHO 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo)).

## Free questionnaires carry the burnout signal, and the MBI is off the table

The chronic-risk view should rest on self-report, and licensing determines which instruments an app can use. The **Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT)** is explicitly "non-proprietary" and free to use without permission, provided it is not modified and Schaufeli, Desart & De Witte (2020) is cited ([burnoutassessmenttool.be](https://burnoutassessmenttool.be/en/)). The BAT manual PDF carries an "all rights reserved" notice, but that concerns reproducing the manual itself, not administering the questionnaire. The **Copenhagen Burnout Inventory's** original release from the Danish NFA prints items and scoring with no licence restriction ([NFA](https://nfa.dk/media/hl5nbers/cbi-first-edition.pdf)). The **Maslach Burnout Inventory is effectively excluded**. Mind Garden licenses it per administration (from $2.75 per copy, 50-copy minimum), for "research data collection only, not for providing individual feedback," and forbids availability "via the open web" ([Mind Garden](https://www.mindgarden.com/173-mbi-license-to-reproduce.html)). The PSS requires a free permission request via Mapi ePROVIDE and has no cut-offs ([CMU lab](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales)). WHO-5 may be used with attribution ([PsyToolkit](https://www.psytoolkit.org/survey-library/who5.html)).

The licence status of the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale, the Recovery Experience Questionnaire and the OLBI could not be verified from primary sources. The KSS is the natural single-item state-sleepiness check for session start, but its licence should be confirmed before embedding. All cut-offs come from Flemish, Danish or Dutch samples, and none are validated for Indian workers. For momentary check-ins, a meta-analysis of experience-sampling studies found **87% compliance at 1–3 prompts a day versus 77% at 4–5**, and the lowest compliance (63%) for prompts with more than 26 items ([Williams et al. 2021](https://www.jmir.org/2021/3/e17023)). A voluntary, unpaid app will likely do worse. The practical defaults are a start-of-day sleepiness and energy check, an end-of-day fatigue rating, and nothing more by default. Self-report also serves as the ground truth for calibrating each person's EEG mapping. A raw EEG ratio cannot be a label for itself.

## Breathing helps a little, and EEG cannot yet confirm recovery

For break content, slow breathing has the clearest evidence. A meta-analysis of RCTs found **stress reductions of g = −0.35** (12 RCTs, 785 adults) and anxiety reductions of g = −0.32, with no established dose-response for session length ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/)). A review of 223 studies found vagally mediated HRV rose during slow breathing, right after a session, and after multi-session programmes ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/38169/)). In the only head-to-head trial, 108 people practised for 5 minutes a day over 28 days. **Cyclic sighing** (two-part nasal inhale, long mouth exhale) improved positive affect more than mindfulness, and all arms reduced anxiety equally ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947)). That trial excluded people with heart disease, seizures, pregnancy, psychosis and related conditions. About 10% reported negative experiences, which argues for gentle cues, optional breath-holds and a non-breathing alternative. Psychological detachment from work correlates with **lower exhaustion (r = −0.36) and fatigue (r = −0.42)** across thousands of workers, though the evidence is correlational ([Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)). A 40-second nature view slightly reduced attention lapses in one study ([Lee et al. 2015](https://Www.ltl.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/40-second-green-roof-views-sustain-attention.pdf)).

Whether EEG can *show* recovery is the weakest link. Fatigue raises theta and alpha, and relaxation and eye closure also raise alpha, so "alpha went up after the break" cannot distinguish recovered from drowsier. In a lab study, a 15-minute rest break **restored reaction time with no significant immediate EEG change**, while an exercise break lowered frontal and parietal theta. The behavioural recovery was transient ([Wang et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9524192)). Only 4 of 15 slow-breathing studies recorded EEG. They were small and unblinded, and their results were mixed: alpha rose in three and theta fell in two ([Zaccaro et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full)). Sham-controlled trials of alpha neurofeedback for anxiety often fail to beat placebo, though that summary comes from a non-peer-reviewed source ([Sapien Labs](https://sapienlabs.org/mentalog/eeg-neurofeedback-anxiety/)). An honest recovery check therefore needs matched recording conditions before and after the break: a 60-second eyes-open segment and a 60-second eyes-closed segment each time, never comparing eyes-closed exercise data with eyes-open work data. Results should be expressed as z-scores against the user's own typical break, paired with a one-item energy rating, and labelled as a "relaxation signal," not proof of recovery. Movement and stretching breaks should not be scored at all, because headphone electrodes pick up motion and EMG.

## Burnout unfolds over months, so trends need weeks of persistence

WHO's ICD-11 defines burnout as "a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed." It has three dimensions (exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism, reduced efficacy), and it is **"not classified as a medical condition"** ([WHO 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases)). Longitudinal panels support exhaustion as the first stage. High exhaustion predicted depersonalisation a year later (β ≈ 0.15–0.18), and cynicism fed back on the other dimensions. The authors warn, however, that the effects are "not large enough to be of practical use in the recognition of burnout," and stability effects dominate ([Taris et al. 2005](https://scispace.com/pdf/are-there-causal-relationships-between-the-dimensions-of-the-1676bbh7l6.pdf)). Over six months, workload predicted later exhaustion and low reward predicted cynicism, and the authors suggest even six months may be too short to see change ([Jimenez & Dunkl 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00012/pdf)). The BAT also lists sleep problems, worry, tension and psychosomatic complaints as secondary symptoms.

The most defensible early-warning signals are therefore sustained rises in **exhaustion, need for recovery and sleepiness**, with rising mental distance as an escalation signal. No validated individual burnout-risk algorithm exists, and no published thresholds for skipped breaks or late-evening work predict burnout. A defensible trend layer works as follows:

- **Personal baseline:** build it from the first four weeks of weekly BAT4 scores.
- **Elevated flag:** raise it when the two-week mean exceeds baseline by about 0.5 points or one personal SD for at least two consecutive weeks. EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average) or CUSUM (cumulative sum) change detection is reasonable for this, but has never been validated for burnout scores.
- **Monthly anchor:** use the BAT-12 traffic light.
- **Three outward states:** OK, watch, and talk to someone. "Watch" covers BAT-12 orange, NFR ≥54.5 or a sustained two-week rise. "Talk to someone" covers BAT-12 red, WHO-5 ≤50, or watch-level signals persisting four weeks.
- **EEG and logs:** EEG fatigue slopes and logged work hours only add context ("your hours rose while your energy fell"). They never raise the risk level on their own, because no evidence links them to burnout.

At "talk to someone," the app should explain the score without diagnosing. It should point to a doctor, the employer's assistance programme or India's free **Tele-MANAS line (14416 or 1-800-891-4416)** ([PIB](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2022057)), and treat a low WHO-5 score as a prompt for a check-up because it may indicate depression.

## Neural data law rewards an app the employer cannot see

In India, the DPDP Rules were notified on **13 Nov 2025**. The substantive notice, consent, rights and breach duties apply from **13 May 2027**. Breach reports to the Data Protection Board are due within 72 hours, and CERT-In separately requires notice within 6 hours ([AZB & Partners](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/update-indias-digital-personal-data-protection-framework-comes-into-effect/)). DPDP has no sensitive-data tier, so EEG gets no special status. Section 7(i) also lets employers process employee data for employment purposes without consent, which commentators say makes consent "the wrong starting point for an employment relationship" ([Sansa Legal](https://www.sansalegal.com/post/how-to-rely-on-the-employment-legitimate-use-under-section-7-i-of-the-dpdp-act)). The practical consequence: an employer-operated EEG deployment would struggle to show genuinely free consent. A truly local app that the employee controls sidesteps that problem.

In the EU and UK, regulators do not treat neurodata as automatically special-category. The UK ICO, however, calls it "high impact and high risk," notes that concentration tracking "could reveal mental health information," and says that in employment, "because of the power imbalance, consent is likely inappropriate in most cases" ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/research-and-reports/ico-tech-futures-neurotechnology/regulatory-issues)). The sharper constraint is the AI Act. **Article 5(1)(f) bans systems that infer emotions from biometric (including physiological) data in the workplace.** Recital 18 excludes physical states such as **fatigue**, and the medical exception "does not extend to general well-being monitoring, such as stress or burnout detection" ([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/eu-commission-issues-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-part-ii)). Whether "stress" counts as an emotion, and whether self-use by an employee escapes the workplace ban, remain open questions that need EU counsel. The safe reading is that fatigue, alertness and sleepiness outputs are fine, while "stressed," "anxious" or "frustrated" labels are a liability. On the medical-device boundary, the FDA's January 2026 wellness guidance allows outputs framed around "sleep, activity, stress, recovery." It disallows naming conditions, "abnormal" labels, clinical thresholds, or claims to diagnose, prevent or treat a disease ([Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/fda-adapts-with-the-times-on-digital-health-updated-guidances-on-general-wellness-products)). Chile's regulator classified the Emotiv Insight as a non-device because of its claims ([Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/)). Claims drive classification, so "prevents burnout" is itself a risky claim.

Neurorights are hardening into law. Chile's Supreme Court ordered Emotiv to **delete a user's brain data in 2023** under constitutional neuro-protections ([Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/)). Colorado (2024), California (effective 2025) and Connecticut (effective July 2026) classify neural data as sensitive data, and Montana extends genetic-privacy protections to neurotechnology data ([Neurorights Foundation briefing](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/Senate%20Health%20and%20Welfare/Bills/H.814/Witness%20Testimony/H.814~Ashley%20Collins~US%20State%20Legislation%20to%20Protect%20Neural%20Data~4-9-2026.pdf)). California's definition excludes data inferred from *non*-neural sources, so EEG-derived scores should be treated as neural data. UNESCO's November 2025 Recommendation requires freely given, revocable consent and prohibits pressure on workers to use neurotechnology. It also bars neural data from manipulative nudging ([Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/)). Workplace precedents show where harm arises. State Grid's reported "emotional surveillance" caps drew sharp scepticism about the science ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2018/04/30/143155/with-brain-scanning-hats-china-signals-it-has-no-interest-in-workers-privacy/)). SAP Focus UX, built with Emotiv, exposed employees' cognitive load and stress to managers, while SmartCap's safety-framed fatigue detection reportedly serves over 5,000 companies ([Duke Magazine](https://dukemag.duke.edu/stories/extent-employee-surveillance-greater-you-know)). In these cases harm came from emotion inference and from data reaching the employer. Fatigue detection itself was the accepted use.

## Design rules that follow from the evidence

The rules below tie each design choice to the evidence above. Numeric values (z-thresholds, caps, retention periods) are design defaults that no study validated. They should be tuned against users' own self-reports.

| Area | Rule | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| EEG scoring | Base fatigue on log theta plus alpha and IAF slowing relative to baseline; drop beta from the denominator | Tran 2020; EMG evidence |
| EEG scoring | Replace "stress" with an explicit EMG/muscle-tension metric (>30–35 Hz) used to grey out beta-based indices | Paralysis studies; Sensors 2024 |
| EEG scoring | Collapse five gauges into 2–3: fatigue/alertness trend, engagement proxy, muscle tension. Attention and relaxation are reciprocals of each other | Shared numerators |
| EEG scoring | Use log power and z-scores, 2–4 s Welch windows, 5–15 min trend smoothing; compare within-day slopes across days rather than absolute levels; re-estimate IAF periodically | Wascher 2018; Benwell 2019; no test-retest data |
| Data quality | Gate on contact quality, amplitude, blinks and EMG; apply a 50 Hz notch; report the percentage of clean data; do not trust vendor "green" contact indicators | Sensors 2024 (Muse "green" but non-physiological) |
| Breaks | Trigger on sustained fatigue (e.g. 3–5 min above personal baseline), with a 50–60 min fallback and a mid-morning anchor break | Albulescu; HSE; Hunter & Wu; Biwer |
| Breaks | Defer during typing and meetings; enforce minimum gaps; cap at ~6–8 prompts a day; always offer snooze, "after this task" and skip; no lockouts; expect ~40% acceptance | Luo 2018; Iqbal & Bailey; Trougakos |
| Breaks | Default to 1–5 min screen-free breaks; suggest ~10 min when fatigue persists; flag working through lunch; show a return-to-work cue | Albulescu; Trougakos; Luo |
| Exercises | Offer cyclic sighing and 6/min paced breathing first, with gentle cues, optional holds and a non-breathing option; exclude hyperventilation protocols | Fincham; Laborde; Balban |
| Recovery check | Use matched eyes-open and eyes-closed pre/post segments against the user's typical break, plus a one-item rating; call it a "relaxation signal"; never say "recovered" | Wang 2022; alpha confound |
| Burnout view | Run BAT4 weekly, BAT-12 monthly, NFR and WHO-5 fortnightly; present BAT items verbatim; exclude the MBI; show OK/watch/talk-to-someone states with multi-week persistence | Licences; Taris; Williams |
| Safety | Never diagnose; at high scores signpost a doctor, the employer assistance programme and Tele-MANAS 14416 | WHO ICD-11; PIB |
| Privacy | Process on-device, discard raw EEG, encrypt derived scores, send no telemetry, require no account, provide one-click export and delete | DPDP Rules; ICO; Chile ruling |
| Privacy | Build no employer dashboard, API or "anonymised team" report; make employer access technically impossible; warn users about installing on employer-managed laptops | Workplace precedents; DPDP s.7(i); ICO on consent |
| Wording | Output only fatigue, alertness, sleepiness and workload; use no emotion labels; avoid "detect/prevent burnout," "anxiety" and "depression"; position the product as a personal tool, not an HR product | AI Act Art. 5(1)(f); FDA wellness guidance |

## Conclusion

The evidence reverses the intuitive design. EEG headphones look like the product's core, but they are its least-validated component for the stated goal. Validated instruments, break science and privacy architecture carry most of the defensible value, and EEG earns its place as a personal fatigue sensor that times breaks and makes within-day strain visible. The same choice that makes the science honest also clears the legal path: reporting fatigue rather than stress or emotions is both what the theta evidence supports and what the AI Act's fatigue carve-out permits. A local-only design is likewise both what neuro-ethics demands and what keeps the vendor outside most data-protection duties. That last point is an inference, since no regulator has addressed employee-only local EEG apps directly.

The open gaps also give the app a research agenda of its own. Day-to-day test-retest reliability of these headphones, whether EEG fatigue indices normalise after a short break, and whether EEG-timed prompts beat a plain 50–60 minute timer are all unanswered. An app that collects paired EEG and self-report data locally, with users' explicit opt-in, is positioned to test them. Until that evidence exists, the app should present every EEG score as approximate, specific to the moment, and relative to the user's own baseline.
