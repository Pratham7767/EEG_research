# Break Timing, Micro-breaks and Work/Rest Schedules for Knowledge Workers (evidence for "smarter break timing" in an EEG burnout-prevention app)

Note on method: PubMed and PMC pages were blocked by a browser check during this session, so some abstracts were read through publisher, university repository or press-release pages. Those are flagged where it matters. About 22 tool calls.

## 1. Micro-break meta-analysis (Albulescu et al. 2022): effects on vigor, fatigue, performance, and break length

### Takeaway
Micro-breaks (10 minutes or less) reliably but modestly raise vigor (d = 0.36) and lower fatigue (d = 0.35). They do not reliably improve performance overall (d = 0.16, not significant). Longer breaks predict bigger performance gains, but break length does not change the well-being effects. For cognitively demanding tasks, performance did not improve.

### Cited Findings
- Scope: 19 publications, 22 independent samples, N = 2,335. Break lengths ranged from 8 seconds to 10 minutes. — [Albulescu et al. 2022, PLOS ONE (PMC9432722)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Pooled random-effects results: vigor d = 0.36 [0.16, 0.55], k = 9, p < .001. Fatigue reduction d = 0.35 [0.19, 0.50], k = 9, p < .001. Performance d = 0.16 [-0.04, 0.37], k = 15, not significant, I² = 59.45%. — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Break duration was a significant moderator only for performance (b = 0.07, p = .006, R² = .34): longer micro-breaks went with larger performance gains. Duration was not significant for vigor (p = .909) or fatigue (p = .436). — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Task type moderated performance. Cognitively demanding tasks: d = -0.09, not significant (k = 9). Clerical tasks: d = 0.56, p = .047 (k = 2). Creative tasks: d = 0.38 [0.11, 0.64] (k = 3). — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Break activity (cognitive, physical, relaxing, nature) could not be tested as a moderator because there were too few studies per category. No included study found breaks harmed performance. — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Limitations: all well-being outcomes were self-reported. Only 4 of 22 samples were at low risk of bias across criteria. Egger's test suggested publication bias for performance (p = .048). Two extreme outliers were removed. The paper is internally inconsistent on some numbers (performance p = .116 vs .17; cognitive-task CI). — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)
- Press coverage of the same paper exists. Use the primary paper for numbers. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/08/220831152754.htm); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/gimme-microbreak)

### Inferences
- The app should present micro-breaks mainly as a fatigue and energy (well-being) intervention, not a productivity booster. Effects of about d = 0.35 are visible but small, so "breaks make you more productive" overstates the evidence for cognitive work.
- Well-being benefits do not depend on length within 10 minutes, so short prompts (1–5 min) are justified for frequent use. If the user's work is clerical or repetitive, or if restoring performance is the goal, a longer break (around 10 minutes) is better.
- For EEG-triggered breaks, the fatigue effect (d about 0.35, self-reported) is the closest outcome measure. It is a reasonable prior for how much a post-break self-report or EEG fatigue index might move.

### Gaps
- No pooled estimate by break activity, and no dose-response for frequency (breaks per hour).
- No physiological (EEG, HRV) outcome was pooled. All well-being outcomes were self-reported.

## 2. Named schedules: Pomodoro (25/5), 52/17, 90-minute ultradian (BRAC), time-on-task vigilance decrement

### Takeaway
Of the popular schedules, only Pomodoro-style fixed intervals have been tested in a controlled study (Biwer et al. 2023, students). Fixed short breaks gave better mood than self-regulated breaks, with the same task completion. 52/17 comes from a vendor's observational analysis that was never peer-reviewed, and the vendor's own 2021 rerun gave 112/26. I found no workplace trial for the 90-minute ultradian rule.

### Cited Findings
- Biwer, Wiradhany, oude Egbrink & de Bruin (2023), *British Journal of Educational Psychology* 93(S2):353–367. 87 university students studying online for one day. Conditions: self-regulated breaks (n = 35), "Pomodoro" 24 min work + 6 min break (n = 25), and short systematic 12 min + 3 min (n = 27). — [Maastricht University CRIS record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)
- Biwer et al. results: self-regulated students had longer study sessions and longer breaks. They reported higher fatigue and distractedness, and lower concentration and motivation, than the systematic-break groups. Invested mental effort and task completion did not differ. The authors concluded fixed breaks had mood benefits and were more efficient (same completion in less time). — [Maastricht CRIS](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)
- A follow-up study compares self-regulated, Pomodoro and "Flowtime" break-taking among students. Its full text could not be retrieved here. — [Maastricht CRIS: Investigating the Effectiveness of Self-Regulated, Pomodoro, and Flowtime Break-Taking Techniques](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/investigating-the-effectiveness-of-self-regulated-pomodoro-and-fl/); [PMC12292963](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)
- 52/17 origin: a 2014 DeskTime (time-tracking vendor) analysis of its top-rated 10% of users, who averaged about 52 minutes of work then about 17 minutes away. It was published as a company blog post, not peer-reviewed. Problems: it selects already-productive users, "productivity" is the software's own app classification, causation is unclear, and an average is not an individual optimum. — [Silicon Canals analysis](https://siliconcanals.com/t-desktime-52-17-work-break-productivity-observational-data/)
- DeskTime's rerun on 2021 data reported 112 minutes of work and 26 minutes of break for top users, which shows 52/17 is not a stable optimum. — [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-desktime-52-17-work-break-productivity-observational-data/)
- "90-minute" work-interval claims mostly appear in popular and business media (Inc., CPA Australia's *In The Black*, productivity blogs) that cite Kleitman's Basic Rest-Activity Cycle (BRAC). They do not cite controlled workplace trials. — [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/wanda-thibodeaux/why-working-in-90-minute-intervals-is-powerful-for-your-body-and-job-according-t.html); [INTHEBLACK](https://intheblack.cpaaustralia.com.au/work-life/body-breaks-and-90-minute-mark)
- In a CHI field study of a break-prompting tool, users who chose their own work interval most often picked 60 minutes (46% of days). — [Luo et al. CHI 2018, "Time for Break"](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)

### Inferences
- Popular claims ranked by evidence: (1) fixed short work/break cycles (Pomodoro-like) have one small controlled study in students, with a mood benefit and no performance cost. (2) The 52/17 and 90-minute rules have no controlled evidence. Present them as heuristics, not science.
- Biwer et al. suggest self-regulated breaks tend to come late, after fatigue has built. That supports app nudges or a schedule fallback over purely self-initiated breaks. It also fits Hunter & Wu's finding that earlier breaks recover more (see section 3).
- A schedule fallback of about 50–60 minutes of continuous work matches HSE guidance ("5–10 minutes every hour"), what users choose themselves in the CHI study, and roughly the 52/17 folk heuristic.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a primary source for Kleitman's BRAC or for daytime ultradian performance cycles. The 90-minute claim should be called unverified for knowledge work.
- I did not retrieve primary vigilance-decrement studies (Mackworth-style time-on-task decrement, typically within 15–30 min in monotonous vigilance tasks). If needed, cite them from a separate source. Knowledge work is not a pure vigilance task, so generalizing is uncertain.
- No controlled study in employed knowledge workers comparing 25/5 vs 50/10 vs 90/20 was found.

## 3. Break timing: early vs late, self-initiated vs scheduled, breaking before fatigue, lunch breaks

### Takeaway
Breaks earlier in the day (mid-morning) gave more resource recovery than late-afternoon breaks (Hunter & Wu, JAP). Autonomy over what to do on the break matters a lot (Trougakos et al., AMJ lunch study). Fixed breaks beat self-chosen ones on mood in students (Biwer 2023). Together: prompt before depletion, but let the user decide the activity and fine-tune the timing.

### Cited Findings
- Hunter & Wu, "Give Me a Better Break: Choosing Workday Break Activities to Maximize Resource Recovery," *Journal of Applied Psychology* 101(2):302–311 (online 2015, issue 2016). 95 university administrative employees, 5-day diary, 959 break surveys. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835); [PubMed 26375961](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26375961/)
- Timing: contrary to the authors' hypothesis, breaks later in the shift were followed by fewer resources (energy, concentration, motivation) and more somatic symptoms. A mid-morning break gave "far more benefit" than a late-afternoon one. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835); [Baylor news release](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2015/new-research-confirms-how-take-better-workday-breaks)
- Activities: breaks with preferred, enjoyable activities recovered the most. Better breaks were linked to fewer symptoms such as eyestrain, headache and back pain, and to higher job satisfaction and less burnout over the week. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835)
- Hunter & Wu effect sizes and the findings on break length and frequency were not reported in the secondary sources I could access. (Press coverage reported that frequent short breaks were better, but I could not verify this in the primary text.) — [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/2258853/need-a-break-from-work-take-it-in-the-morning-says-one-study)
- Trougakos, Hideg, Cheng & Beal, "Lunch breaks unpacked: The role of autonomy as a moderator of recovery during lunch," *Academy of Management Journal* (2014). Administrative employees, 10 workday diaries, end-of-day fatigue rated by coworkers. — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/768564); [PolyU record](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/lunch-breaks-unpacked-the-role-of-autonomy-as-a-moderator-of-reco/)
- Trougakos et al. findings: freely chosen relaxation at lunch gave the lowest fatigue. Working through lunch raised end-of-day fatigue unless the person chose it. Socializing also raised fatigue when it was not autonomous, for example with colleagues or the boss. Autonomy was the key moderator. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/768564)
- HSE (UK regulator) says users should ideally be able to choose when to take breaks, and if work has no natural changes of activity, employers should schedule breaks. — [HSE DSE: Work routine and breaks](https://www.hse.gov.uk/Msd/dse/work-routine.htm)
- Students on self-regulated breaks reported more fatigue and less concentration and motivation than students on fixed breaks. — [Biwer et al. 2023](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel)

### Inferences
- "Take breaks before fatigue" is supported indirectly: early breaks recover more, and self-regulated breakers end up more fatigued. Primary evidence that breaks taken before vs after a fatigue threshold differ is lacking. The app should not wait for strong EEG fatigue before suggesting the first break of the day. A schedule-based first break by mid-morning is defensible.
- Autonomy is a moderator, not optional. Prompts should offer choice (activity, "in 5 min", "after this task") rather than forcing a lockout. Forced or monitored breaks may lose some of the recovery benefit (inferred from the lunch study's autonomy effect).
- Lunch: encourage a real, user-chosen, relaxing lunch away from work. Flag working through lunch as a risk pattern (it adds to fatigue), especially if the user didn't choose it.

### Gaps
- No primary-source effect sizes for Hunter & Wu or Trougakos et al. (PubMed and full text blocked).
- No trial was found that directly compares app-scheduled vs user-initiated breaks in employed knowledge workers.

## 4. Break activities that work: detachment, relaxation, social, physical, screen use

### Takeaway
Relaxing, preferred and autonomously chosen activities recover best. Socializing helps only when it is voluntary and with people you can relax around. Phone use during breaks is linked to more afternoon emotional exhaustion despite similar detachment (correlational). Movement breaks are the regulatory default for screen work.

### Cited Findings
- Preferred and enjoyable break activities predicted better recovery of energy, concentration and motivation. — [APS summary of Hunter & Wu](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122835)
- Relaxation at lunch gave the least end-of-day fatigue. Non-autonomous socializing and working raised fatigue. — [EurekAlert, Trougakos et al.](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/768564)
- Rhee & Kim (2016), *Computers in Human Behavior* 57:160–167. 425 Korean workers, single-day survey (correlational). Lunch phone users reported about the same psychological detachment as walkers or chatters, but more emotional exhaustion in the afternoon. The usual link between detachment and fewer negative feelings was missing in heavy phone users. Reverse causation is possible. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/put-it-away-spending-work-breaks-your-smartphone-not-rejuvenating); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/why-you-should-break-up-with-your-smartphone-during-lunch-breaks.html)
- A separate experimental study compared "conventional" and "smartphone" breaks for regaining vitality at work. Full text was not retrieved. — [Kim et al., "Effects of breaks on regaining vitality at work: An empirical comparison of 'conventional' and 'smart phone' breaks"](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1816076731)
- HSE: breaks or changes of activity should let users get up from the workstation and move around, or at least stretch and change posture. — [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/Msd/dse/work-routine.htm)
- In the CHI break-prompting study, actual breaks (mean 5.92 min) were significantly longer than intended (4.42 min, p < .001), often because of chatting with coworkers. — [Luo et al. CHI 2018](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)
- The Albulescu meta-analysis included cognitive, physical, relaxing and nature micro-breaks but could not rank them. — [Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)

### Inferences
- Break menu defaults: (a) stand, move or walk, (b) look away from screens and relax (breathing, window or nature view), (c) voluntary social contact. Discourage switching to the phone or social media. That is not "detachment" in recovery terms, even if it feels like it.
- For an EEG app: a screen-free break also avoids contaminating the post-break EEG reading with screen-viewing artifacts. If the app wants to measure recovery, ask the user to step away from screens.
- A "return to work" cue is useful because breaks overrun their intended length.

### Gaps
- Sonnentag's detachment and recovery literature (Recovery Experience Questionnaire: detachment, relaxation, mastery, control) was not fetched in this session. Cite separately if needed.
- No effect sizes for phone vs non-phone breaks from primary sources.

## 5. Interruption cost, flow, notification design, JITAI frameworks, ergonomics guidance (HSE/OSHA, 20-20-20)

### Takeaway
Interrupting at task boundaries is much cheaper than interrupting mid-task (Iqbal & Bailey). Break prompts should wait for natural breakpoints, pause during active typing, and offer postponement. Real users decline or ignore most prompts (60% in a CHI field study), mainly because they are busy or in meetings. Use a JITAI framing: decision points, tailoring variables (EEG fatigue, receptivity), options that include "do nothing", and decision rules. For eyes, the 20-20-20 rule has small-trial support for symptom relief only while reminders keep running.

### Cited Findings
**Interruption timing**
- Iqbal & Bailey (CHI 2006): with interruptions only at subtask boundaries, resumption lag still differed by boundary type. High-cost boundaries ≈ 1,702 ms, medium ≈ 1,012 ms, low ≈ 464 ms (log means 7.44 / 6.92 / 6.14; F(2,131) = 25.23, p < .0001; n = 12 users in evaluation). — [Iqbal & Bailey CHI 2006 PDF](https://interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI06-p741-iqbal.pdf)
- That paper, summarizing earlier work, says interrupting at random moments rather than at boundaries can mean up to 30% longer resumption, up to twice the errors, and up to twice the negative affect. — [Iqbal & Bailey CHI 2006](https://interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI06-p741-iqbal.pdf)
- Iqbal's Microsoft Research talk abstract reports favorable results for deferring notifications to meaningful task breakpoints. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=183260)
- Framework and system work on breakpoint-based notification scheduling: OASIS. — [Microsoft Research: Oasis](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=161181); [Iqbal & Bailey framework (IDEALS)](https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/83111); [Iqbal & Horvitz CSCW 2010](https://www.erichorvitz.com/shamsi_iqbal-eric_horvitz_cscw_2010.pdf)
- Microsoft Research has modeled "opportune moments for transitions and breaks at work" (full text not reviewed). — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/02/Modeling-Opportune-Moments-for-Transitions-and-Breaks-at-Work.pdf?lang=fr_ca)
- A survey of intelligent notification systems is available. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi, arXiv 1711.10171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)

**Break-prompt field evidence (CHI)**
- Luo et al. "Time for Break" (CHI 2018): 25 information workers, 3 weeks. Prompts came after continuous computer use reached a user-set duration, were delayed 15 s while the user was typing or clicking, and closed after 2 min if unanswered. Of 1,599 prompts: 40% "yes", 34% "no", 26% ignored. — [Luo et al. CHI 2018](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)
- Reasons for "no" (n = 525): busy working 40.6%, meeting or class 18.9%, just had a break 15.2%, close to finishing something 8.8%, leaving soon 7.9%, in a conversation 3.7%. — [Luo et al.](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)
- Users found prompts sometimes annoying or surprising, felt guilty saying "no", and short work intervals made pop-ups too frequent. Pre-existing habit strength predicted "yes" (OR = 1.26, p < .001). Consistent break durations built more habit. — [Luo et al.](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)
- Their design implications: support a consistent rhythm, add a return-to-work prompt, use calendar, to-do and phone context to pick better moments, give adherence feedback, and use 25/55-minute meeting defaults. — [Luo et al.](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/%7echoe/download/CHI-2018-Luo-TimeforBreak.pdf)

**JITAI**
- JITAI components (Nahum-Shani et al.): decision points, tailoring variables, intervention options (including postponing or doing nothing), decision rules, and proximal and distal outcomes. Receptivity is "the person's ability to receive, process, and use the support," treated as a tailoring variable. — [Künzler et al., "Detecting Receptivity for mHealth Interventions in the Natural Environment," arXiv 2011.08302](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302); [JITAI-related PMC article surfaced in search (content not verified, PMC blocked)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5870794)
- In that study (83 participants, 3 weeks), timing messages with a static machine-learning receptivity model raised just-in-time response by 38% vs control (p < .001) and overall response by about 12%. Control-group response declined over the study. The adaptive model's gains were not significant (a software bug limited it to n = 61). Predictors: time of day, weekday/weekend, phone lock state and time since unlock, battery, Wi-Fi, physical activity. — [arXiv 2011.08302](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)
- NIH OBSSR guidance on designing JITAIs, and micro-randomized trials (MRTs) as the standard way to optimize decision rules. — [NIH OBSSR](https://obssr.od.nih.gov/news-and-events/news/how-design-best-just-time-mobile-interventions); [MRT design paper, arXiv 2107.03544](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03544)

**Ergonomics guidance**
- HSE (UK): the law requires employers to plan DSE work so there are breaks or changes of activity. There is no legal break length or frequency. "Take short breaks often, rather than longer ones less often". "5 to 10 minutes every hour is better than 20 minutes every 2 hours". Users should ideally choose when. Break-monitoring software can remind users but doesn't replace the employer's duty. — [HSE DSE: Work routine and breaks](https://www.hse.gov.uk/Msd/dse/work-routine.htm); [HSE FAQ](https://www.hse.gov.uk/contact/faqs/vdubreaks.htm)
- 20-20-20 rule trial: Talens-Estarelles et al. (2022/2023), *Contact Lens & Anterior Eye*. 29 symptomatic computer users got 2 weeks of webcam-tracked, personalized 20-20-20 reminders. Digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms fell (p ≤ .045) but returned 1 week after reminders stopped. No change in dry eye signs or most binocular measures, except higher accommodative facility (p = .010). Reminders increased break count and shortened work bouts (p ≤ .015). — [Aston University record](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effects-of-breaks-on-digital-eye-strain-dry-eye-and-binocular/); [PubMed 35963776](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35963776/)
- A separate paper questions whether the 20-20-20 numbers are justified. — ["20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified?" (BVS record)](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/ses/resource/pt/mdl-36473088)

### Inferences
- Interruption policy: when an EEG trigger fires during active input (typing or clicking, app focus inside a document), defer until an idle gap or window or task switch (a coarse breakpoint), up to a maximum deferral. Luo's 15 s typing deferral is a minimal version.
- Expect roughly 40% acceptance as a realistic baseline. Measure and optimize toward it rather than treating declines as failure. Model "just had a break" and "in a meeting" explicitly (calendar integration, recent idle detection) to remove about a third of "no" responses.
- Prompt fatigue is real (the control arm's response fell over time in Künzler et al.). Receptivity-aware timing and fewer, better-timed prompts beat fixed frequent prompts.
- The 20-20-20 benefit depends on continued reminders, so eye micro-prompts must be ongoing, unobtrusive and low-cost (non-modal), not a one-time education.
- OSHA (US): I found no specific OSHA break-interval rule for computer work. HSE is the most concrete regulatory guidance.

### Gaps
- Mark, Gudith & Klocke (CHI 2008, "The cost of interrupted work") and the often-quoted "23 minutes to resume" were not retrieved. Treat the "23 minutes" claim as unverified popular lore unless sourced.
- No primary source retrieved on alert or notification fatigue thresholds (prompts per day before habituation) specific to break reminders.
- OSHA computer workstation eTool content was not retrieved.

## 6. Long hours and overtime (WHO/ILO 2021)

### Takeaway
Working 55 or more hours a week is linked to a 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from ischemic heart disease, vs 35–40 hours. That accounted for an estimated 745,000 deaths in 2016. This is the strongest evidence-based "hard ceiling" signal for a burnout app.

### Cited Findings
- WHO/ILO estimates (2016): 745,000 deaths from stroke (398,000) and ischemic heart disease (347,000) attributable to working 55+ hours a week. — [WHO news release, 17 May 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo)
- 55+ h/week vs 35–40 h/week: about 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from IHD. About 9% of the global population works long hours. Deaths rose 29% from 2000 to 2016 (IHD +42%, stroke +19%). — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo); [ILO](https://www.ilo.org/global/about-the-ilo/newsroom/news/WCMS_792131/lang--en/index.htm)
- Underlying systematic reviews and estimates: Pega et al. 2021, *Environment International*. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/404a4276f13b4fb1b4ce034dd2f89861)

### Inferences
- The app should track weekly active hours and escalate (stronger messaging, suggestions to protect evenings and weekends) as weekly totals approach 50 h and especially at 55+ h. These are population risk estimates, not individual predictions.

### Gaps
- Dose-response between 40 and 55 h, and links to burnout specifically (vs cardiovascular outcomes), were not retrieved.

## Concrete design defaults (synthesis; evidence strength in brackets)

**Trigger logic (hybrid JITAI)**
1. **Physiological trigger:** prompt when the EEG fatigue or stress index stays above the user's personal baseline (for example, z > 1.0–1.5 against a rolling 7–14 day personal baseline) for at least 3–5 minutes. Use a sustained window, not a momentary spike. [Inference: no published validated EEG threshold for break prompting was found. Personal baselining follows JITAI tailoring practice.]
2. **Schedule fallback:** if no physiological trigger fires, prompt after 50–60 minutes of continuous active computer use. [HSE "5–10 min every hour"; users' modal choice of 60 min in CHI 2018; Pomodoro-type fixed breaks beat self-regulated breaks on mood (Biwer 2023)]
3. **Early-day anchor:** ensure one proactive break by mid-morning (for example, around 90–120 min after the start of work) even if signals look fine. [Hunter & Wu: earlier breaks give more recovery]
4. **Self-report as a confirm and calibrate channel:** a one-tap "how depleted are you? 1–5" on some prompts, used to recalibrate the personal EEG threshold. [Inference]
5. **Long-hours escalation:** weekly active-time warnings at about 45 h, with strong messaging at 50–55 h. [WHO/ILO]

**Timing and anti-nagging**
- Defer prompts during active typing or clicking. Deliver at the next breakpoint (idle of 5 s or more, app or window switch, file save or send), with a maximum deferral of about 10 minutes. [Iqbal & Bailey; Luo 2018's 15 s typing delay]
- Suppress during calendar meetings, screen sharing, presentations and full-screen apps. [Luo 2018: 18.9% of declines were "in a meeting"]
- Minimum gap between prompts: 20–30 minutes after any prompt, and 45 minutes after a completed break, so "just had a break" declines (15.2%) disappear. Treat detected idle of 5 minutes or more as a break and reset the timer. [Inference from Luo 2018]
- Daily cap: about 6–8 break prompts (one per working hour), plus at most 2 extra if EEG strongly indicates them. Back off (lengthen intervals) after 2 consecutive declines, and recover gradually. [Inference. Prompt fatigue seen in Künzler et al. control arm]
- Always offer "Snooze 10 min", "After this task", and "Skip". Use non-modal UI by default and no forced lockout. [Autonomy: Trougakos et al.; HSE "users should ideally choose"]
- Expect about 40% acceptance as the baseline KPI. [Luo 2018]

**Break length**
- Micro-break: 1–5 minutes (default 3) for EEG-triggered or hourly prompts. [Albulescu: vigor and fatigue effects don't depend on length within 10 min or less]
- Longer break: about 10 minutes if a sustained high-fatigue state persists after a micro-break, or for clerical or repetitive work where performance matters. [Albulescu: longer breaks give bigger performance gains, b = 0.07]
- Lunch: encourage 30 minutes or more, user-chosen, relaxing, away from the desk. Flag working through lunch. [Trougakos et al. AMJ]
- Eyes: optional non-modal 20-second look-away nudge about every 20 minutes during long screen bouts, kept running because benefits fade within a week of stopping. [Talens-Estarelles et al.; small n = 29]
- Return-to-work cue at the end of the intended break. [Luo 2018: breaks overran, 5.9 vs 4.4 min]

**Break content**
- Default suggestions: stand or walk, stretch, look away from screens or out a window, slow breathing. Allow a voluntary social option. Discourage phone or social media during breaks. [HSE; Hunter & Wu preferred activities; Rhee & Kim 2016 correlational]
- Let users pick favorites. Preferred activities recover best. [Hunter & Wu]
